Introduction

In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate.

Sometimes, in the midst of life’s turmoil, a person pauses and asks themselves questions that their heart cannot ignore:

Why am I here? Where do I come from? Where am I going? What is the true purpose of my existence?

Deep within every soul lies this need for meaning, this search for truth, and this longing for something that transcends this fleeting world

Islam is a response to this deep call from the heart. It reminds us that we are not here by chance, that every breath is a blessing, and that our existence was willed by Allah, our Creator.

He teaches us that, despite our weaknesses, our mistakes, and our trials, the door to Allah’s mercy always remains open to those who sincerely turn back to Him

Islam is thus much more than a religion: it is a path that guides the soul when it feels lost, a light in times of darkness, and a source of peace when the heart is troubled. It is about getting to know Allah, loving Him, placing one’s trust in Him, and understanding that every trial can bring us closer to Him

Islam is thus much more than a religion: it is a path that guides the soul when it feels lost, a light in times of darkness, and a source of peace when the heart is troubled. It is about getting to know Allah, loving Him, placing one’s trust in Him, and understanding that every trial can bring us closer to Him

To speak of Islam, then, is to speak of a return to what truly matters: to our Creator, to the truth, and to the peace that the heart has always sought

The word ‘Islam’ is generally translated as ‘submission’ or ‘surrender to God.’ The one God is called Allah, an Arabic term that simply means ‘God’

The primary sacred text is the Quran, which Muslims regard as the word of God revealed to Muhammad in Arabic. The second major religious source is the Sunnah, which compiles the teachings, sayings, and practices attributed to the Prophet Muhammad, particularly through the hadiths.

Muhammad

According to Islamic tradition, Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570

At the age of 40, around 610, he received his first revelations through the angel Gabriel (Jibrîl).

He then began to preach:

the existence of one God

the rejection of idolatry

the moral responsibility of each individual

solidarity with the poor and orphans

justice

the Last Judgment

His message is meeting with significant opposition in Mecca.

The Hijra: 622

In 622, Muhammad and his companions left Mecca for Yathrib, which would later become Medina.

This event, known as the Hijra, is fundamental to Muslim history: it marks the beginning of the Islamic calendar

In Medina, Muhammad became a religious leader, a political leader, and an arbiter of the community

Return to Mecca

Muhammad died in Medina in 632.

Upon his death, much of the Arabian Peninsula converted to Islam or joined his political alliance.

After Muhammad : The first Caliphs

After his death, the question arose as to who would lead the Muslim community.

The first four leaders are traditionally known as the “Rightly Guided Caliphs” (al-khulafāʾ al-rāshidūn):

1 - Abou Bakr — 632-634

2 - Omar ibn al-Khattab — 634-644

3 - Othman ibn Affan — 644-656

4 - Ali ibn Abi Talib — 656-661

It was during this period that the Muslim state expanded rapidly beyond Arabia, particularly toward:

Syria;

Egypt;

Persia;

Iraq.

But this period was also marked by serious political conflicts.

Sunnis and Shiites: Why This Division?

The division between Sunni and Shia Islam stems in particular from the question of Muhammad's succession.

- Some Muslims believed that Ali, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, should play a special role in the succession.

- Others recognized the first caliphs, notably Abu Bakr

- With the political conflicts that followed, this divergence gradually evolved into a deeper religious and theological distinction.

The central event in Shi'ism is the Battle of Karbala in 680, during which Hussein ibn Ali, Muhammad's grandson, was killed.

5. The Five Pillars of Islam

The Five Pillars (arkān al-islām) constitute the main fundamental practices of Sunni Islam.

1- The Shahada — the declaration of faith

There is no god but God, and Muhammad is the Messenger of God.

In arabic : Lā ilāha illā Allāh, Muḥammad rasūl Allāh.

It is an affirmation of monotheism and Muhammad's prophetic mission.

2- Salat — Prayer

Muslims perform five daily prayers:

at dawn;

at noon;

in the afternoon;

at sunset;

in the evening.

Prayer is performed facing Mecca.

It is a daily opportunity to connect with God and practice spiritual discipline.

3- Zakat — the obligatory almsgiving

Zakat is a form of mandatory charity intended primarily for those in need. In its traditional form, it generally amounts to 2.5 percent of certain eligible assets, provided the required conditions are met.

It therefore has one dimension:

* spiritual;

* social;

* economic.

The idea is, in particular, to ensure that wealth is distributed fairly and to strengthen community solidarity.

4- Le Sawm — Ramadan fasting

During the month of Ramadan, adult Muslims who are able to do so abstain from eating and drinking from dawn until sunset.

Fasting, however, is not limited to food: it is also associated with self-control, prayer, generosity, and spiritual reflection.

Exemptions apply, in particular, to certain people with medical conditions, travelers, and certain special circumstances.

5- The Hajj — the pilgrimage to Mecca

Every Muslim who is physically and financially able must perform the pilgrimage to Mecca at least once in their lifetime.

Every year, the Hajj brings together Muslims from all over the world.

In particular, it symbolizes:

* the unity of believers;

* equality before God;

* the remembrance of Abraham (Ibrahim), Hagar (Hajar), and Ishmael (Isma'il).
